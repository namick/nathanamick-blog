Course Plan & Operating Manual · Living Document

The Writing Course
2026–2027

An interest-driven 8th grade writing curriculum, built at home, taught one nameable technique at a time.

Version 1.2 · August 6, 2026 · Companion to Teaching Writing at Home: Research Notes · Calendar matched to the local school district (first day Aug 3, last day May 28, 180 days)

This document is the course. It defines the year's goals, the quarter-by-quarter map with due dates, and — most importantly — the protocols used to run it day to day: how each daily handout gets generated, how completed work gets evaluated, and how the course gets steered as we learn what's working. Individual lessons are not pre-written; they are created each morning from the recipe in §6, aimed at whatever the skills tracker (§5) says matters right now. That is by design: the research says responsive teaching beats a fixed script, and it keeps the course steerable all year.

§1How to use this document

  • Daily: open a session with the AI, reference this plan, and generate the handout(s) for today (and tomorrow, if printing ahead) using the protocol in §6. Customize on the spot — swap the topic, shrink the assignment, lean into whatever she's currently obsessed with.
  • After each assignment: her finished work goes in writing/completed/. Run the evaluation protocol in §7 to produce her feedback note and update the skills tracker.
  • At each milestone date: check the quarter map (§4). Milestones are the fixed points; everything between them flexes.
  • Whenever the course needs to change: use §8. Edit this document, bump the version, note the change in §9. The plan serves the writer, not the other way around.

Standing rule for every lesson, all year: each lesson teaches one nameable technique, shows it working in a mentor text, then has her use it immediately with a specific product goal. Assigning writing is not teaching writing. If a handout doesn't have a technique you can name in five words, it isn't ready to print.

§2Course goals — what June looks like

By the end of May 2027, the student will have:

  1. Written and published in all three modes. At least one polished narrative, one informative/research piece, and one full argument essay, each taken through the complete process (plan → draft → feedback → revise → edit → share with a real audience). This covers the full 8th grade standard.
  2. A repeatable writing process she can describe. She can say how she plans, what she does when a draft stalls, and what she looks for when revising — the explicit-strategy outcome the research ranks first.
  3. Sentence-level command. Comma splices gone from final drafts; varied sentence openers; comfortable use of subordinate clauses and because/but/so reasoning. Built through daily five-minute drills, never worksheets.
  4. Clean dialogue and paragraphing. Correct dialogue punctuation and deliberate multi-sentence paragraphs — her two most mechanical growth areas from the August baseline.
  5. Show-don't-tell as a habit, not a trick. Emotions dramatized through action, dialogue, and sensory detail rather than named.
  6. A capstone she's proud of — a sustained Q4 project of her choosing (illustrated storybook, animation script + storyboard, or collected works), printed or produced for a real audience.
  7. An intact love of writing. The non-negotiable. She started this year voluntarily writing 1,100-word stories with subverted endings; the course must end with that enthusiasm larger, not smaller. Every protocol below (limited feedback, choice of topics, real audiences) exists partly to protect this.

Q1 Narrative & Description — Story Craft Mon Aug 3 – Fri Oct 2 · 44 days

The point of this quarter: she already writes complete stories with real arcs and revises voluntarily. Q1 sharpens the craft inside that enthusiasm: dramatizing instead of naming emotion, varying sentences, handling dialogue mechanics, and building real paragraphs — while establishing the daily rhythm and the feedback culture.

Technique menu (each becomes one or more lessons)

  • Show-don't-tell: actions, body, and dialogue instead of named emotions (Lesson 3, already prepared — her #1 growth area)
  • Dialogue mechanics: punctuation, capitalization, tags, new-speaker-new-paragraph
  • Sentence openers: subordinate-clause and participial starts to break the "She… She… The dragon…" pattern
  • Sentence combining to kill comma splices (taught in her own sentences, e.g. "She really wanted to go forward but she couldn't, she was frozen with fear")
  • Paragraphing on purpose: what goes together and why; the one-sentence-paragraph as a deliberate effect, not a default
  • Pacing: stretching the important moment, compressing travel
  • Endings: earned reflection vs. summary; how her subverted-ending instinct works and when to deploy it
  • Reading like a writer: pull apart a paragraph from a book she loves (HTTYD novels, current reads) — one move per session

Q2 Informative / Explanatory — Research & Explanation Mon Oct 19 – Tue Dec 22 · 44 days

The point of this quarter: writing that teaches a reader something, using more than one source. Summarization (the research's #2 practice) lives here. The trick is keeping it interest-driven: ferrets, insects, Minecraft, and how animation works are all legitimate research topics.

Technique menu

  • Summarizing a source in her own words — taught explicitly, practiced weekly
  • Note-taking without copying; quoting vs. paraphrasing
  • Judging sources: who wrote this and how do they know?
  • Organizing structures: compare/contrast, cause/effect, sequence, problem/solution
  • Formatting for readers: headings, captions, intro/conclusion moves
  • Precise vocabulary and formal-but-alive tone
  • Sentence work continues: appositives ("the ferret, a member of the weasel family, …") are the Q2 drill star

Q3 Argument — Claims, Evidence, Counterclaims Tue Jan 5 – Fri Mar 12 · 47 days

The point of this quarter: the 8th grade summit — state a claim, support it with evidence, and take the other side seriously enough to answer it. Start with arguments she has actual stakes in; formality comes last.

Technique menu

  • Claim vs. opinion vs. fact; what counts as evidence
  • Because/but/so at essay scale: reasons that actually support the claim
  • The counterclaim move: "someone might say… but…" — steelmanning, not strawmanning
  • Formal tone without going dead; cutting "I think" and "very"
  • Evidence from texts: citing the actual film/book/source, not vibes
  • Structure: hook, claim, reasons in strongest-last order, counterclaim, conclusion that doesn't just repeat
  • Sentence drills: subordinating concessives — although, even though, while — the counterclaim's grammar

§5The skills tracker

The tracker is the course's steering signal: a short list of current teaching points, drawn from her actual work, that daily drills and lesson choices aim at. It lives here and gets updated by the evaluation protocol (§7). Keep it to 3–5 active items — more than that and nothing gets focused attention. When an item stops appearing in her drafts for three consecutive pieces, retire it (move it to "Secured") and promote something new.

Secured skills (name them in feedback; keep them warm)

  • Complete story architecture — plans before drafting, real arcs
  • Willing revision — genuinely improves drafts without being forced
  • Narrative risk-taking — subverted endings, emotional beats
  • Natural dialogue content (the words, if not yet the punctuation)
  • Voluntary volume — meets or exceeds word counts unprompted

§6Lesson generation protocol — how each handout gets made

Lessons are generated the morning of (or the night before), one or two at a time, by you + the AI, using this recipe. This is the section to reference when you sit down and say "make today's handout."

Every handout follows this template

  1. Header: lesson number (continuous — next is Lesson 4), date, and the technique named in plain words as one plain sentence ("Making fear visible without saying 'afraid'").
  2. Warm-up (10 min minimum): one sentence drill targeting an active tracker item (§5), built from content she currently cares about — her own story, her current book, dragons, ferrets, Minecraft.
  3. Freewrite (10 min minimum): on every handout, every day. Never graded — stream-of-consciousness with no rules to follow — but turned in with the day's work; one prompt offered, her own choice always wins.
  4. Mini-lesson: the technique explained briefly, then shown working in a short mentor-text excerpt — ideally from a book she loves. One "how did the author do this?" question.
  5. Writing Assignment: one labeled section ("Writing Assignment: ‹name›") holding the whole task as complete sentences, with specific, countable requirements, never "write well." Good: "Write the scene so we know Clara is terrified without using afraid, scared, terrified, or fear. Include at least two sensory details. Aim for 400–600 words." Bad: "add more description."
  6. Checklist: 3–4 concrete success criteria she can self-check before handing it in. These same criteria drive the evaluation in §7.
  7. Stretch: one harder variation, printed as a normal part of the day — the handout never labels it optional ("optional", "only if", "if you want"). The parent decides on the day whether it's skippable and tells her directly.

Voice and format rules

  • The handout talks to the student, directly and warmly, in a calm teacher's voice — playful at most once per page and never in a heading; never babyish, never corny. She's a smart 8th grader who reads.
  • Sized to her, not to a generic 8th grader. Volume is the engine: writing itself is the best writing teacher, reading a close second. The assignment must genuinely fill the writing block for a writer of her demonstrated volume — the floor comes from the Challenge dial in STATUS.md "Parent notes" (authoritative, read every run). If she could finish in ten minutes, it was too small: raise the word floor, add a required technique, or deepen the revision demand — never pad with busywork.
  • One page when possible, two max. Printable: black-on-white friendly, room to write if the task is short.
  • Use her interests as material, not as decoration — a drill sentence about ferrets is good because it's a good drill, not because a ferret clipart is on the page.
  • On full-process-piece days, the "handout" may just be a planning sheet, a revision checklist, or a feedback-response sheet — the template flexes.
Daily generation — run the skill Run /lessons in a project session. It reads this plan (current quarter card in §4, this template, the steering rules in §8), STATUS.md, and the log tail, picks the technique the tracker most needs, and writes the handout as a self-contained printable HTML file in writing/lessons/ — HTML only, no PDFs are created or kept; print from the browser (Cmd-P) when needed. Variants: /lessons 3 for a coherent mini-sequence · /lessons 2 she's obsessed with Wings of Fire, lean into it for count + steering · /lessons redo N <reason> to regenerate an existing handout in place.

Customization is expected at this step, every day — swap the mentor text, change the topic to this week's obsession, halve the assignment on a low-energy day, or abandon the plan entirely because she asked a great question yesterday. The technique-menu items are the checklist; the order and flavor are yours.

§7Evaluation protocol — what happens to completed work

Completed assignments land in writing/completed/ (established format: planning answers + rough draft + completed version in one file). Then, together with the AI:

  1. Evaluate against that lesson's checklist criteria (headed "Checklist"; lessons 4–7 used "You nailed it if…") — not against everything writing could ever be. Did she use the technique the lesson taught?
  2. Scan against the skills tracker. Which active items showed up? Which didn't? Any candidate for retirement, or a new pattern worth adding?
  3. Produce the feedback note for the student. The fixed format:
    • One genuine strength, named precisely, with her exact words quoted back. ("'I suppose if you must,' said with fear — that line does characterization and plot at once.")
    • One teaching point. One. Usually the next lesson's seed.
  4. Log a one-line evaluation note (date, piece, criteria met, tracker observations, next-lesson seed) so the next generation session can read the trail.

What we deliberately don't do: mark every error (teaches that writing is error-avoidance), grade individual pieces (feedback beats grades in the research), or fix her sentences for her (the comma splices fall to focused drills, not red ink). Freewrites and journals are never evaluated at all.

Quarterly review

In the last week of each quarter: re-read the quarter's completed folder together, pick her best piece and her favorite piece (they may differ — good conversation), write a short growth memo (what improved, with before/after quotes; what's next), and update the tracker and next quarter's plan. If a grade is ever needed for records, it's assigned holistically from the quarter's body of work at this review — mastery of the quarter's techniques, completion of milestones — not averaged from pieces.

§8Steering the course

This plan is a starting map, and the territory will disagree with it. Rules for changing it:

  • Fixed: the weekly rhythm (§3), the one-technique-per-lesson rule, the feedback format, the three modes each getting a quarter, and the capstone due dates.
  • Flexible by design: everything else — technique order, project topics, piece lengths, which mentor texts, intermediate milestone dates (slide up to a week without ceremony; note it in §9).
  • If she's ahead: don't accelerate the calendar — deepen. Longer pieces, harder mentor texts, an extra genre inside the quarter's mode (poetry, comics script, letters).
  • If a milestone is slipping: shrink the piece, not the process. A 400-word story that goes through plan-draft-feedback-revise-share teaches more than a 1,500-word draft that dies in a folder.
  • If enthusiasm dips: that's a course emergency ahead of any milestone. Drop to freewrites + reading like a writer for a week, change the project topic to the current obsession, and add an audience (mail something to someone). Volume follows interest; skill follows volume.
  • Mid-quarter check (calendar it, ~week 5 of each quarter): Is she writing more or less than in August? Which lesson did she talk about at dinner? What's she reading, and is it in the lessons yet?
