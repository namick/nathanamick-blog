Teaching Writing at Home

Research notes for building an 8th grade writing curriculum · August 1, 2026

These notes summarize what the research says about teaching adolescent writers, what existing curricula do well (and whether anything free is worth adopting), what the student's first two assignments reveal, and a sketch of how a year-long homemade curriculum could be structured. The short version: the research strongly supports the approach you're already taking — a homemade, interest-driven curriculum with frequent writing and a real reader — as long as it also includes explicit strategy instruction and deliberate sentence-level work.

1. What the research actually says

The most-cited evidence base for teaching writing to students in grades 4–12 is Writing Next (Graham & Perin, 2007), a Carnegie-commissioned meta-analysis that screened 582 documents and drew on 123 of them, yielding 154 effect sizes for writing quality. It ranked eleven instructional practices by effect size. The top of the list, in order of impact:

  1. Explicit strategy instruction — directly teaching step-by-step strategies for planning, drafting, and revising (the single largest effect, especially the "Self-Regulated Strategy Development" (SRSD) approach, which teaches a strategy, models it aloud, then gradually hands it over to the student).
  2. Summarization — explicitly teaching how to condense a text.
  3. Collaborative writing — working with someone else through the process (in a homeschool: you, a sibling, or a co-op friend).
  4. Specific product goals — "add three sensory details to this scene" beats "make it better."
  5. Word processing — typing measurably helps adolescent writers revise more.
  6. Sentence combining — merging short, choppy sentences into varied, complex ones. Notably, this replaces traditional grammar drill, which had a negative effect in the studies. Grammar taught in the context of the student's own sentences works; isolated worksheet grammar doesn't.
  7. Prewriting, inquiry, process approach, model texts — brainstorm before drafting; study excellent examples of the kind of writing being attempted and imitate their moves.

The headline finding: writing improves fastest when students are explicitly taught how to do each part of the process — not just given prompts and time. Assigning writing is not the same as teaching writing. Every lesson should teach one nameable technique, then have her use it.

The sentence-level revolution

The second big idea in current writing pedagogy comes from Judith Hochman's The Writing Revolution (the "Hochman Method"). Its core claim: most struggling-to-average adolescent writers don't need more essay practice — they need deliberate practice at the sentence level: expanding kernel sentences, combining sentences, using conjunctions like because / but / so to force reasoning, and starting sentences with subordinating phrases. Read the evidence honestly: the components are well supported (sentence combining carries an effect size of 0.50 in Writing Next, and the What Works Clearinghouse names sentence construction in its elementary practice guide, grades 1–6, at moderate evidence). The packaged method itself has not been independently trialed, so treat it as a well-reasoned bet on well-evidenced parts. Sentence work takes five minutes a day and transfers directly into longer pieces.

Other well-supported principles

  • Write frequently, at varying lengths. Daily low-stakes writing (journals, freewrites) plus a longer piece taken through the full process every week or two.
  • Feedback beats grades. Specific, limited feedback — one genuine strength named precisely, plus one teaching point — improves writing more than marking every error. Marking everything teaches students that writing is about avoiding mistakes.
  • Authentic audience matters. Writing that will actually be read by someone (family read-alouds, a printed booklet, a blog, a letter that gets mailed) produces measurably more effort and revision than writing that goes into a folder.
  • Choice and interest drive volume, and volume drives skill. This is the homeschool superpower — a class of 30 can't let everyone write about ferrets and dragons. You can.
  • Read like a writer. Regularly pull apart a paragraph from a book she loves: "How did this author show fear without saying 'afraid'?" Mentor texts are among the cheapest, most effective tools available.

2. What 8th grade writing is supposed to cover

Standards (Common Core and most state equivalents) expect an 8th grader, by year's end, to write in three modes, plus foundations:

ModeWhat it means at 8th grade level
NarrativeReal or imagined stories with dialogue, pacing, description, and reflection that feels earned; deliberate use of transitions and sensory detail.
Informative / explanatoryExamine a topic using organized facts, definitions, and details drawn from more than one source; formatting (headings), precise vocabulary, formal tone.
ArgumentState a claim, support it with reasons and evidence from credible sources, acknowledge and respond to a counterclaim, maintain formal style.
FoundationsMulti-paragraph organization, revision with audience in mind, command of conventions (this is the year comma splices should get fixed), short research projects, and using a word processor fluently.

A homemade curriculum that cycles through these three modes — with narrative and descriptive work first, since that's where her enthusiasm is — will cover everything a boxed curriculum would.

Conclusion: your instinct is right. There is no free curriculum better than what you can build: an interest-driven, mentor-guided course that borrows explicit strategy instruction from SRSD, sentence-level drills from Hochman, and the enthusiasm-first culture of Brave Writer.

4. What her first two assignments show

Strengths to build on

  • Complete story architecture. Her dragon story has a real arc — inciting incident, rising tension, climax, aftermath — and she planned it before drafting.
  • Willingness to take risks. She deliberately chose a tragic ending that subverts reader expectations ("she will fail to help the last dragon") — that's sophisticated narrative thinking for day two.
  • She actually revises. The completed version genuinely improves on the rough draft: merged choppy lines, smoothed transitions, fixed "tore down" → "torn down." Revision is usually the hardest habit to instill, and she already has it.
  • Natural dialogue and emotional beats. "I suppose if you must," said with fear, is a genuinely good line.
  • Voluntary length. Both pieces meet or exceed the word counts without prodding.

Growth areas (this year's teaching points)

  • Comma splices and run-ons — "She really wanted to go forward but she couldn't, she was frozen with fear." Fix through sentence-combining work, not grammar worksheets.
  • Telling instead of showing — emotions are frequently named ("with fear in his voice," "Clara was shocked," "he said emotionally") rather than dramatized. Lesson 3 targets this directly.
  • Repetitive sentence openings — long runs of sentences starting "The dragon..." / "Clara..." / "She...". Subordinate-clause openers are the fix.
  • Dialogue conventions — capitalization and punctuation around quotes ("can I try to help?", "The dragon said").
  • Paragraphing — the draft is mostly one-sentence paragraphs; she began merging them in revision, which shows she's ready for explicit paragraph instruction.

5. A sketch for the year

A structure to react to — four quarters, each anchored in a mode, all customizable to her interests:

QuarterAnchor modePossible interest-driven projects
Q1Narrative & descriptionStory craft: show-don't-tell, sentence variety, dialogue mechanics, paragraphing, revision habitsAn original short story (her own world, not a continuation); a "creature field guide" with illustrated entries; rewrite a scene from HTTYD from another character's point of view
Q2InformativeResearch and explanation: summarizing sources, organizing by structure (compare/contrast, cause/effect), precise vocabulary, formattingA real research report on ferrets or an insect; a Minecraft modpack review or how-to guide; "how animation works" explainer tied to her animation goal
Q3ArgumentClaims, evidence, counterclaims, formal tone"Why animals X should/shouldn't be kept as pets"; a persuasive pitch for something she actually wants; a review arguing the best HTTYD film with evidence from the films
Q4Capstone / multi-genreSustained project taken through the full process with publicationAn illustrated storybook, a script + storyboard for a short animation (writing meets her animation goal), or a printed "collected works" of the year

A weekly rhythm the research supports

  • Daily (10 min): low-stakes freewrite or journal — never graded, occasionally shared.
  • Daily (5 min): one sentence-level exercise (combine, expand, because/but/so, new opener) — Hochman-style, using content from whatever she's reading or writing.
  • 2–3×/week: a lesson + assignment like the three you have now, each teaching one nameable technique.
  • Every 1–2 weeks: one piece goes through the full process — plan, draft, feedback, revise, edit, share (read aloud at dinner, print it, send to a grandparent).
  • Feedback protocol: name one specific thing that worked and why, then one teaching point. Resist fixing everything; the comma splices will fall to focused instruction, not red ink.
